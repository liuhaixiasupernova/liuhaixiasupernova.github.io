Mock Exam

Six written-answer questions, 40 marks in total. There are no multiple-choice questions. Work with pen and paper like in the exam: write in full sentences, draw your diagrams, then compare and mark with others. Talking through why an answer earns marks is where most of the learning happens.

Practice only. Doing well here does not predict your exam mark, and the real exam will contain different questions.

How to use this mock exam

  1. Attempt it alone first, on paper. Use a pen, work in exam conditions, and leave nothing blank. Wrong attempts teach more than empty pages. The sketch pads below are for quick diagrams if you are on a tablet or phone, but paper is best.
  2. Peer-mark. Swap papers with a partner. Mark in a different colour of pen. For each answer, write what earned marks and what is missing, in words, not just a number. Explaining a mark to someone else tests your own understanding.
  3. Group-mark and discuss. Join another pair. For every question, compare your answers and diagrams and agree what a full-mark answer contains. Disagree politely: ask "why does that earn a mark?" and "what would change if the problem were different?".
  4. Check against the hints last. Open the "Hints and marking prompts" only after you have discussed, then add anything the group missed.
  5. Teach it back. Pick the question you found hardest and explain it to someone without looking at your notes. If you get stuck, that is exactly what to revise.

Marking is not about matching phrases. Examiners give marks for correct ideas, applied to the problem in the question, with a reason. The aim is not a high score on this page, it is to understand why each answer works.

Question 1[5 marks]

Using diagrams, show how a random hillclimber can experience difficulties searching a problem space and then describe a way you can improve upon its simple approach.

Peer and group discussion: Compare diagrams with your partner. Do you both show a hill the climber cannot get off? Whose improvement is easiest to explain, and why does it help?

Hints and marking prompts (open after you have discussed)
  • Draw a landscape with more than one peak and show where the climber starts and stops.
  • What does it do when no neighbouring move is better?
  • Name a way to change how it starts, how it moves, or what it accepts.

Show a likely population of four parents after selection using the roulette-wheel scheme, indicating why. How might the population look if you use another form of selection? Indicate why.

Peer and group discussion: Check each other\'s working: do your wheel slices match the fitness shares? Is "likely" different from "certain" in your answers? Agree why another selection method would change the population.

Hints and marking prompts (open after you have discussed)
  • Work out each solution's share of the total fitness. That is the size of its slice of the wheel.
  • The result is random, so give a likely outcome and say why it is likely, not a certain one.
  • Compare with a method that depends on rank or on small contests, and say what changes.

Question 3[5 marks]

How well might you expect Particle Swarm Optimisation to perform on each of the two problem spaces below and why?

Peer and group discussion: Take one problem space each and argue for or against good performance, then swap. Which statement would convince a sceptic, and what evidence would you want?

Hints and marking prompts (open after you have discussed)
  • What do the particles do as they move toward each other and toward the best place found so far?
  • Where can that take them in each picture?
  • Is there a way to change who sees whom, or another method that makes bigger jumps?

Question 4[5 marks]

Using two examples, describe the basic principles behind swarm intelligence algorithms.

Peer and group discussion: Each person picks a different example and describes it without the words "leader" or "controller". Then compare: what do your two examples have in common?

Hints and marking prompts (open after you have discussed)
  • Who is in control? What does a single agent know?
  • How do the agents influence one another, directly or through the world?
  • What appears at the level of the whole group that no single agent contains?

Hints and marking prompts (open after you have discussed)
  • Where is the best result, and is it in the middle of the table or at an edge?
  • If it is at an edge, what lies beyond it that has not been tested?

Hints and marking prompts (open after you have discussed)
  • Careful: this time the best result is the lowest number. Find it first.

What mistake would someone make in (b) if they read it like (a)? Agree one sentence of reasoning that would earn the marks in each.

Question 6[5 marks]

The graph below shows the performance over time of an evolutionary algorithm on a maximization problem. What parameter or parameters would you change to try to improve the quality of the final solution found and why?

Peer and group discussion: Describe what each curve does to a partner who cannot see the graph. Then agree on one change and the effect it should have, and say how you could test it.

Hints and marking prompts (open after you have discussed)
  • Describe what each curve is doing, and what the gap between them tells you.
  • Link the pattern to a setting: mutation, selection pressure, population size, elitism.
  • Say which way you would change it and what you expect to happen.
